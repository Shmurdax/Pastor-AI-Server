## Foreword

I love Christmas.
I always have.
It is, without question, my favorite time of the year. I love the lights. I love the gifts. I love the decorations. I love the music. I love the feeling that seems to settle over everything when Christmas begins to draw near.
And yes, I am one of those people, I decorate early. October is not too early for me! The moment I can begin pulling out the decorations and turning on the Christmas lights, something inside of me comes alive.
There is just something about Christmas.
I love walking into a store and hearing Christmas carols playing overhead. I love driving through neighborhoods glowing with lights. I love seeing homes decorated, families gathering, gifts being wrapped, trees going up, and children filled with anticipation.
But somewhere along the way, I realized there was something deeper stirring in me.
I don't simply love Christmas.
I love WHO Christmas is about.
Behind every light is the reminder of the Light who stepped into our darkness.
Behind every gift is the reminder of the greatest Gift ever given.
Behind every song is the echo of the night heaven filled the skies with worship.
Behind the manger is a mystery almost too wonderful to comprehend:
God came near.
That is the awe and wonder of Christmas.
The more I have studied the Christmas story, the more amazed I have become by what actually happened in Bethlehem. Christmas did not begin with Mary and Joseph traveling to Bethlehem. It did not begin when the angel Gabriel appeared to Mary. It did not even begin with the prophecies announcing that a Messiah would come.
Christmas was already in the heart of God.
Long before there was a manger, there was a plan. Long before there was a star, there was a promise. Long before the shepherds heard the angels, heaven already knew what was coming.
And when the fullness of time arrived, the plan became a Person.
The Word became flesh.
God stepped into our world.
Immanuel came.
God with us.
That truth is at the heart of this book. As I wrote these pages, I found myself falling in love with the Christmas story all over again, not merely with the familiar details, but with the revelation hidden within them. The manuscript traces Christmas from God's eternal plan through the incarnation and repeatedly returns to this central reality: Jesus is the message God sent to humanity.
There is awe in Mary saying yes.
There is wonder in Joseph choosing obedience.
There is purpose in Bethlehem.
There is revelation in the star.
There is significance in the manger and the swaddling clothes.
There is a message in the shepherds.
There is worship in the heavenly hosts.
There is pursuit in the wise men.
And through it all, there is Jesus.
The Ancient of Days became an infant. The eternal stepped into time. The One who holds all things together allowed Himself to be held in the arms of a young mother. The omnipotent became dependent. Heaven touched earth, and everything changed.
That is why I never want Christmas to become so familiar that I lose the wonder of it.
I never want to hear the story of Bethlehem and simply think, I already know this.
I want to stand beside the shepherds and be amazed again. I want to look toward Bethlehem and wonder again. I want to hear the angels announce the good news and worship again.
I want to see the Child and remember again:
This is Jesus.
The Savior.
The Messiah.
The Word made flesh.
The Gift heaven gave to earth.
Immanuel—God with us.
So, for the next twenty-five days, I invite you to slow down. Look again. Listen again. Read the familiar story with fresh eyes.
Maybe the lights will look a little different this year.
Maybe the songs will sound a little different.
Maybe when you hear His name playing over the speakers in a crowded store, something inside of you will stop and remember:
They're singing about Jesus.
In the middle of shopping lists, Christmas parties, wrapping paper, decorations, family gatherings, and all the beautiful busyness of the season, don't miss WHO Christmas is all about.
Because Christmas is more than a holiday to celebrate.
It is more than a story to remember.
It is the breathtaking announcement that God came near.
And my prayer is that somewhere within these pages, you will discover again...the awe and wonder of Christmas.

## Introduction

Advent is the season when time slows down long enough for us to remember that God has always been speaking. Long before shepherds heard angels, long before a virgin heard Gabriel, long before a manger held the Son of God, the story of Christmas was already alive in the heart of the Father. “He chose us in him before the creation of the world” (Ephesians 1:4 NIV). Bethlehem was not an interruption in history—it was the unveiling of a plan older than creation itself.
Scripture tells us that God’s Word is not merely sound or syllable.
It is intention. It is logic. It is blueprint. It is plan. “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God” (John 1:1 NIV). The Logos was not simply spoken; the Logos was—eternal, deliberate, and moving toward a moment when the invisible would become visible. And when that moment finally arrived, heaven called it a rhema—a thing spoken, a thing accomplished, a thing now standing in the world as the result of God’s own utterance. The shepherds said, “Let’s go to Bethlehem and see this thing that has happened” (Luke 2:15 NIV).
Advent invites us to stand where they stood and behold the Word made flesh. “The Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us” (John 1:14 NIV). To trace the plan of God from eternity into time. To watch the mystery unfold page by page, prophecy by prophecy, until the plan becomes a Person and the Word becomes flesh.
This 25-day devotional journey is an invitation to rediscover the awe and wonder of Christmas—not as sentiment, but as revelation. Day by day, you will walk through the language of God, the message of God, the mystery of God, and the timing of God.
You will see how Scripture whispers His name, how prophecy leans toward His coming, and how history bends toward the fullness of time. “But when the set time had fully come, God sent his Son” (Galatians 4:4 NIV).
Christmas is not merely a story to remember. It is a message to receive. A message spoken in the language of a Son. “In these last days he has spoken to us by his Son” (Hebrews 1:2 NIV). A message that still breaks silence, still interrupts darkness, still brings hope to weary hearts.
As you read, may the eternal plan of God become fresh to you again. May His message speak deeply to your heart. May the mystery of Christ unfold before your eyes. And may the awe and wonder of Advent draw you closer to the One who came near—
Immanuel, God with us.
“And they will call him Immanuel” (which means “God with us”)
(Matthew 1:23 NIV).

## December 1: This Thing

Luke 2:15 NIV: “When the angels had left them and gone into heaven, the shepherds said to one another, ‘Let’s go to Bethlehem and see this thing that has happened, which the Lord has told us about.”
The shepherds had heard the angelic announcement. They had seen the night sky blaze with glory. Then the angels returned to heaven, and the fields grew quiet again. But heaven had left them with something they could not ignore: “this thing that has happened.”
That phrase opens a beautiful window into the language of God.
In the New Testament, two Greek words often translated as
“word” help us understand this moment more clearly: Logos and Rhema. Both matter. Both reveal something about how God speaks. And on the first night of Advent, both point us to Jesus.
Rhema speaks of an utterance, a saying, a matter spoken, or a thing made known. It is the expressed word—the word that is heard, received, and responded to. In Scripture, rhema can describe what has been spoken and what now stands before us as reality.
Logos carries the sense of word, message, reason, thought, and divine expression. It is more than sound; it is meaning. It is the thought behind the speech, the purpose behind the declaration, the wisdom behind the plan. John uses this word when he writes,
“In the beginning was the Word” (John 1:1 NIV).
When we understand the Logos as the eternal Word, the divine reason, and the plan of God, John 1 becomes even more wondrous. Jesus is not simply part of God’s plan; He is the plan revealed. He is the Father’s heart made visible, the wisdom of God embodied, and the eternal Word made flesh.
From the beginning, God created with purpose. Nothing about creation was random.
Nothing about redemption was improvised. Bethlehem was not a last-minute rescue mission. The Lamb was known before the foundation of the world, and the Son was always the center of the Father’s eternal purpose.
That means the birth of Jesus was not simply a beautiful moment in history. It was the unveiling of what had always been in the heart of God. The plan that existed in eternity stepped into time.
The invisible became visible. The promise became personal.
So when John calls Jesus the Logos, he is revealing more than a title. Jesus is the living expression of God’s mind, heart, wisdom, and will. He is the message God had always intended to send. He is the Word through whom all things were made and the Word by whom all things are redeemed.
John 1 may be read with this wonder in mind: in the beginning was the eternal purpose of God, and that purpose was with God, and that purpose was God. All things came into being through Him. Then, in the fullness of time, the Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us.
The plan did not remain hidden in eternity. The Logos became visible in Bethlehem. The eternal Word took on skin, breath, and bone. The wisdom of God became a baby. The plan of God became a Person.
The Plan Became a Person
That is why Luke 2:15 is so powerful. When the shepherds said,
“Let’s go to Bethlehem and see this thing that has happened,”
they were not merely going to inspect an event. They were running toward the visible fulfillment of what God had spoken.
The Rhema had happened. The Logos had come. The message of heaven was lying in Bethlehem.
Rhema
“Let’s go see this Rhema that has happened.”
What God had spoken was no longer only announcement. It was fulfillment. It had entered the world in the Person of Jesus Christ.
The shepherds were invited to see what heaven had declared. The ones who watched lambs in the night were summoned to behold the Lamb of God. They were not simply told to believe an idea;
they were invited to witness a fulfillment.
And they went quickly. Wonder moved their feet. Revelation demanded a response. Once they heard, they had to see. Once they saw, they had to tell. The Word made flesh became their testimony.
This is where Advent begins for us too. We do not come to Bethlehem as spectators. We come as worshipers. We come to behold the plan of God fulfilled in the Person of Jesus Christ. We come to receive the Word who still speaks, still saves, and still comes near.

## December 2: The Message

I have always loved the beauty of language. Sign language speaks through movement. Spanish carries music in its rhythm. An accent from another country can make even ordinary words sound full of wonder. Language is one of the ways we reveal what is hidden inside us.
God is a speaking God. From the beginning, He has never been silent. But what language does He use?
Psalm 19 tells us that creation itself carries His voice: “The heavens declare the glory of God; the skies proclaim the work of his hands” (Psalm 19:1 NIV). Every sunrise, every sunset, and every star-filled night reminds us that God knows how to make Himself known.
He spoke to Moses through a burning bush. He spoke to Israel through fire and smoke on the mountain. He spoke to Elijah in a still small voice, to Isaiah through a vision, to Hosea through family circumstances, to Amos through a basket of summer fruit, to Jeremiah through a potter’s clay and an almond branch, and to Joseph through dreams. God even spoke His message through a donkey.
God has spoken through visions and dreams, through angels and symbols, through natural events and ordinary circumstances. His revelation is never monotonous. He is not confined to one method, one moment, or one place. He knows how to reach the human heart, and He knows how to make His will known.
Yet every word, every sign, every symbol, and every promise was leading somewhere. All of God’s speaking throughout history was preparing the world for the clearest message He would ever send.
God’s Greatest Message Is Jesus
John calls Jesus the Logos: “In the beginning was the Word”
(John 1:1 NIV). Jesus is not merely another messenger in a long line of messengers. He is the message itself, the Word made flesh, the exact expression of the Father’s heart.
A word communicates. A word reveals what is hidden inside the speaker. In the same way, Jesus reveals the heart of God. If you want to know what the Father is like, look at the Son. If you want to hear what God is saying, listen to Jesus. If you want to understand the message of heaven, behold Christ.
Hebrews 1:1–4 TPT says, “Throughout our history God has spoken to our ancestors by his prophets in many different ways.
The revelation he gave them was only a fragment at a time, building one truth upon another. But to us living in these last days, God now speaks to us openly in THE LANGUAGE OF A SON, the appointed Heir of everything, for through him God created the panorama of all things and all time. The Son is the dazzling radiance of God’s splendor, the exact expression of God’s true nature—his mirror image! He holds the universe together and expands it by the mighty power of his spoken word.
He accomplished for us the complete cleansing of sins, and then took his seat on the highest throne at the right hand of the majestic One. He is infinitely greater than angels, for he inherited a rank and a Name far greater than theirs.”
Jesus is the language of God. He is God’s love translated into flesh, mercy made visible, grace made touchable, truth made personal, and salvation made near.
Bethlehem was not only the birth of a child. It was the arrival of the Father’s message in the language of a Son. God did not send a partial message. He sent the fullness of His heart.
When you look into the meaning of Jesus’ name, you begin to see the fullness of the message God sent:
• The First and Last, the Beginning and the End!
• He is the keeper of Creation and the Creator of all!
• He is the Architect of the universe and the Manager of all times.
• He always was, He always is, and He always will be.
• He is unmoved, unchanged, undefeated, and never undone!
• He was bruised and brought healing!
• He was pierced and eased pain!
• He was persecuted and brought freedom!
• He was dead and brought life!
• He is risen and brings power!
• He reigns and brings Peace!
• Isaiah called Him:
• Wonderful Counselor
• Mighty God
• Everlasting Father
• Prince of Peace
He is light and love, holiness and kindness, wisdom and power.
His ways are right. His Word is eternal. His will is unchanging.
He is our Redeemer, our Savior, our Guide, our Comfort, our Joy, our Lord, and our Peace.
When we are weak, He is strong. When we are lost, He is the way.
When we are afraid, He is courage. When we are wounded, He heals. When we are broken, He restores. When we face uncertainty, He remains faithful.
From the beginning of the world to its end, there is no place we can look and not see the fingerprints of Christ. He is before all things, and in Him all things hold together. The message God sent in Jesus is not fragile. It holds creation, history, redemption, and our lives together.
Some messages change our lives in a moment. A diagnosis. A phone call. A loss. A family crisis. A disappointment we did not see coming. One message can shift the ground beneath our feet.
But Advent reminds us that there is another message—one stronger than fear, deeper than grief, and more final than any report this world can deliver. The Father has spoken through His Son. And what He has spoken over us in Jesus is life, hope, peace, forgiveness, nearness, and rescue.
He has said, “Never will I leave you; never will I forsake you”
(Hebrews 13:5 NIV). That is the message beneath every other message. God has come near, and He will not abandon His people.

## December 4: The Fullness of Time

Timing is everything.
Luke 2:6–7 KJV: “And so it was, that, while they were there,
THE DAYS WERE ACCOMPLISHED that she should be delivered. And she brought forth her firstborn son, and wrapped him in swaddling clothes, and laid him in a manger; because there was no room for them in the inn.”
The birth of Jesus did not happen randomly. It happened at the right moment, in the right place, through the right people, according to the perfect timing of God. Luke says the days were accomplished. Not merely passed. Not simply completed.
Accomplished.
Think of everything that had to come together for that holy night:
Caesar’s decree, Mary’s obedience, Joseph’s dream, the journey to Bethlehem, the full inn, the available stable, the angels, the shepherds, the star, the magi, the gifts, the warning dreams, the escape to Egypt, and the return to Nazareth. Every detail moved in rhythm with the purpose of God.
The coming of Christ into the world was not chance, coincidence, or divine improvisation. It was the unfolding of a plan established before the foundation of the world. Heaven was not reacting to history; history was responding to heaven.
A pagan emperor issued a decree at just the right moment. A young virgin carried the Son of God. A righteous man chose obedience over fear. A crowded inn made room for a manger. A small town fulfilled an ancient prophecy. A silent night became the doorway of redemption.
Galatians 4:4–7 ESV: “But when the FULLNESS OF TIME had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law, to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons. And because you are sons, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, ‘Abba! Father!’ So you are no longer a slave, but a son, and if a son, then an heir through God.”
God Moves in the Fullness of Time
Scripture helps us understand time in more than one way. There is chronos—the time we measure in seconds, minutes, hours, days, and years. Chronos is clock time. Calendar time. The time of appointments, deadlines, delays, and waiting rooms.
Most of us live very aware of chronos. We watch the clock. We count the days. We measure how long something has taken and wonder how much longer we will have to wait. But God is not limited by the clocks we watch or the calendars we keep.
There is also kairos—appointed time, God’s time, the decisive moment when His purpose breaks into history. Kairos is not merely time passing; it is time fulfilled. It is the moment when eternity steps into time and something changes because God has acted.
Bethlehem was a kairos moment. Prophecy, promise, longing, silence, empire, obedience, and heaven all converged in one holy night. The cry of a baby broke four hundred years of prophetic silence, and the fullness of time had come.
We live in a world of clocks and calendars, timestamps and deadlines. We cannot escape chronos. But Advent reminds us that chronos is never the whole story. Beneath every ordinary day, God is able to move in kairos moments—holy moments when He says, “This is the time.”
The first messianic promise in Genesis 3:15 had waited across generations. Isaiah’s prophecy of the virgin birth had stood for centuries. Then came four hundred years of silence between the Old Testament and the New. No prophetic word. No public announcement. Only waiting.
But silence does not mean absence. Delay does not mean denial.
Waiting does not mean God has forgotten. All through those quiet years, God’s plan was still moving toward Bethlehem.
Then suddenly, chronos and kairos collided. A baby’s cry broke the silence. The perfect timing of God arrived in one holy moment as the Father sent His Son, born of a woman, born under the law, to redeem those under the law.
This is the wonder of Advent: God not only came at the right time;
He continues to come to us at the right time. Just when we need Him most, He is near. Just when hope feels delayed, His faithfulness is still at work.
What is on God’s timetable for your life? What promise feels delayed? What prayer feels unanswered? What season feels longer than you expected?
You are not forgotten. You are not overlooked. You are on God’s calendar today.

## December 5: Prophecies Fulfilled

Prophecy is one of the clearest ways Scripture reveals the sovereignty of God. Long before Bethlehem, God spoke through prophets, promises, symbols, and signs. Then, in the fullness of time, every word began to take on flesh in Jesus Christ.
The birth, life, death, and resurrection of Jesus were not accidents of history. They were the fulfillment of what God had already declared. The prophets spoke from their own time, but the Spirit was pointing forward to Christ.
Micah, writing centuries before Jesus was born, named Bethlehem as the birthplace of the Messiah: “But you, Bethlehem Ephrathah… out of you will come for me one who will be ruler over Israel” (Micah 5:2 NIV). Out of all the cities and villages in the world, God had already marked the place where Christ would enter history.

Jesus.
Psalm 22 described His suffering with startling detail, including pierced hands and feet and garments divided by casting lots. Long before crucifixion became a Roman instrument of execution,
Scripture was already pointing toward the cross.
Malachi also spoke of the Lord coming to His temple: “Then suddenly the Lord you are seeking will come to his temple”
(Malachi 3:1 NIV). Jesus came while the temple still stood, fulfilling what had been spoken before its destruction in A.D. 70.
Zechariah spoke of betrayal connected to thirty pieces of silver, and Matthew records Judas returning that same amount after betraying Jesus. The details are too precise to be dismissed as coincidence. They reveal the hand of a God who knows the end from the beginning.
There are hundreds of Old Testament prophecies, patterns, and promises that find their fulfillment in Jesus. Consider just a few:
1. The Messiah would be born in Bethlehem. (Micah 5:2)
2. The Messiah would be betrayed for thirty pieces of silver.
(Zechariah 11:12–13)
3. The Messiah’s clothes would be divided and gambled for.
(Psalm 22:18)
4. The Messiah’s hands and feet would be pierced. (Psalm
22:16)
5. The Messiah’s bones would not be broken. (Psalm 34:20)
6. The Messiah would come from the tribe of Judah. (Genesis
49:10)
7. The Messiah would be called out of Egypt. (Hosea 11:1)
8. The Messiah would be buried in a rich man’s grave. (Isaiah
53:9)
Even a small sampling of these fulfilled prophecies should fill us with awe. God did not speak vaguely. He spoke with precision. He did not merely hint at redemption. He announced it, preserved it, and fulfilled it in Christ.
One well-known illustration pictures the odds this way: imagine covering the entire state of Texas two feet deep with silver dollars, marking one coin, stirring all the coins together, blindfolding a person, and asking him to find the marked coin on the first try.
The point is simple: human chance cannot explain what God fulfilled with such accuracy.
The fulfillment of prophecy is not meant merely to impress our minds. It is meant to anchor our faith. The God who spoke through Micah, Isaiah, David, Zechariah, Malachi, and Hosea is the same God who keeps His promises today.
The birth of Jesus proves that God is not guessing, reacting, or scrambling to repair history. He is Lord over history. He sees the end from the beginning. He watches over His Word to perform it.
That means the promises of God are not fragile. They are not wishful thinking. They are not religious optimism. They are rooted in the character of the One who cannot lie and cannot fail.
“See, the former things have taken place, and new things I declare; before they spring into being I announce them to you.”
Isaiah 42:9 NIV

## December 6: The Incarnation of Christ

The incarnation is the wonder at the heart of Christmas: the eternal Son of God took on human flesh.

The Word Became Flesh

## December 7: Immanuel

Immanuel means “God with us.” Few names carry more comfort, nearness, and wonder. It tells us not only who Jesus is, but what God desires: to come near, to dwell among His people, and to make His presence known.
The name appears in Isaiah’s prophecy concerning the Messiah:
“The virgin will conceive and give birth to a son, and will call him Immanuel” (Isaiah 7:14 NIV). Centuries later, when Joseph discovered that Mary was carrying a child, an angel appeared to him in a dream and revealed that this child was the fulfillment of Isaiah’s ancient promise.
Matthew explains it clearly: “All this took place to fulfill what the Lord had said through the prophet: ‘The virgin will conceive and give birth to a son, and they will call him Immanuel’ (which means ‘God with us’)” (Matthew 1:22–23 NIV).
God With Us
The name itself carries the message of Advent. In its simplest sense, Immanuel gathers three beautiful truths into one holy declaration: God with us.
1. Im points to nearness, association, presence, and togetherness.
2. Nu means “us,” reminding us that God’s nearness is personal and relational.
3. El is the word for God. Immanuel declares that the God of heaven has come near to be with us.
Immanuel does not mean God merely watched from above, visited from a distance, or stood among us as one face in a crowd.
It means God entered our world in the Person of Jesus Christ. The eternal Son came near enough to be seen, heard, touched, followed, and known.
This is the wonder of Christmas: God’s desire has always been to dwell with humanity. From Eden to the tabernacle, from the temple to the manger, from the cross to the empty tomb,
Scripture reveals a God who keeps moving toward His people.
Immanuel means God with us, but through the finished work of Christ and the presence of the Holy Spirit, it also becomes God in us. The One who came near in Bethlehem now dwells within His people. We are not abandoned. We are not alone. We are the dwelling place of God.

Advent invites you to receive the comfort of Immanuel—not as a distant doctrine, but as a present reality. The God who came near has not withdrawn His presence.

## December 8: Nazareth

Nazareth was the town where Mary and Joseph lived. It was also the place where the angel Gabriel appeared to Mary and announced that she would carry the Christ child (Luke 1:26).
It was the town where Jesus grew up, the place of His childhood, and the home to which Mary, Joseph, and Jesus returned after their flight to Egypt (Matthew 2:23; Luke 2:39). Bethlehem was the place of His birth, but Nazareth became the place of His hidden years.
Throughout His ministry, Jesus was often called Jesus of Nazareth. Yet Nazareth was not a place of prestige. It was an unwalled, unprotected town with a questionable reputation—a place many would have dismissed as small, ordinary, and insignificant.
So why was He known as Jesus of Nazareth instead of Jesus of Bethlehem, where He was born, or Jesus of Capernaum, where He later ministered? Matthew gives us the answer:
Matthew 2:23 NIV: “He went and lived in a town called Nazareth. So was fulfilled what was said through the prophets, that he would be called a Nazarene.”
Matthew points us back to a prophetic theme woven through Scripture. Isaiah 11:1 says, “A shoot will come up from the stump of Jesse; from his roots a Branch will bear fruit” (NIV).
The Hebrew word often connected to this imagery is netzer, meaning branch or shoot. It describes tender new life rising from what appears cut down, dormant, or forgotten. Nazareth carried that prophetic echo: the Branch would come from a place no one expected.
Think of a tree reduced to a stump. For a season, it appears lifeless. Then one day, a small shoot begins to rise. It is tender, easily overlooked, and yet it carries life within it.
Isaiah said the shoot would come from the stump of Jesse. Jesse was the father of King David, and from David’s line came the kings of Israel. But after generations of rebellion, exile, and foreign rule, David’s royal line seemed dormant. The throne looked like a stump.
For centuries, no Davidic king ruled in Jerusalem. The promise still stood, but outwardly it looked quiet. Then, in the fullness of time, life rose from the stump. A Branch was born. The King came—not from a palace, but through humility, hiddenness, and
Nazareth.
That makes Nathanael’s question in John 1:46 even more meaningful: “Can anything good come from Nazareth?” The answer is yes. More than good came from Nazareth. The Branch came from Nazareth. The Savior came from the place others dismissed.
The Branch from an Overlooked Place There is one more beautiful connection. Isaiah 61:1–2 speaks of the Anointed One who would proclaim good news, bind up the brokenhearted, declare freedom for captives, and comfort all who mourn.
Jesus later read that very passage in a synagogue in Nazareth and declared, “Today this scripture is fulfilled in your hearing” (Luke
4:21 NIV). The place people overlooked became the place where Jesus announced His mission.
Nazareth teaches us that hiddenness is not the same as insignificance. A place can be small in the eyes of people and still be central in the purposes of God. A season can feel quiet and still be full of preparation. A life can seem ordinary and still be carrying promise.
This is the comfort of Advent: God knows how to bring life from what looks cut down, forgotten, or overlooked. He knows how to raise a Branch from a stump and reveal glory from Nazareth places.

## December 9: Mary

Mary was a young woman from Nazareth, engaged to a carpenter named Joseph and preparing for an ordinary future. She was likely dreaming of marriage, home, family, and the quiet rhythms of life in her village. Then, in a single holy moment, everything changed.
The angel Gabriel appeared to her with a message she could never have imagined. Heaven had chosen her womb to carry the Son of God. Before Mary ever answered, before she understood how it could happen, God spoke a word over her identity.
Luke 1:28 NIV: “The angel went to her and said, ‘Greetings, you who are highly favored! The Lord is with you.’”
The phrase “highly favored” comes from the Greek word charitoō, which means to bestow grace, to make accepted, to endue with favor, or to honor by grace. Gabriel was not announcing that Mary had earned a position. He was declaring what God had graciously placed upon her life.
Mary was favored, but favor did not mean her path would be easy.
God’s favor did not remove cost, questions, risk, or surrender. It meant the Lord was with her in the assignment He had given her.
Charitoō is a word of grace. It points to an action God performs, not an achievement we produce. Favor begins in His heart before it ever rests on our lives. Mary’s story reminds us that grace is not earned by the extraordinary; grace makes ordinary people available for extraordinary purposes.
Think of favor like water poured out from above. The vessel does not create the water; it receives what has been given. Mary received grace, and that grace became the atmosphere in which her obedience would grow.
This word appears only rarely in the New Testament, but its meaning reaches all the way into our own lives. Paul uses the same idea in Ephesians when he writes that God has made us accepted in the Beloved.
Ephesians 1:6 carries this same beautiful idea: “To the praise of the glory of his grace, wherein he hath made us accepted in the beloved” (KJV).
That means Mary’s story is not only about the grace placed upon her life; it also points us to the grace God has placed upon ours.
In Christ, we are accepted in the Beloved.
Paul explains why in Ephesians 1:5–6: “Having predestinated us unto the adoption of children by Jesus Christ to himself, according to the good pleasure of his will… he hath made us accepted in the beloved” (KJV).
We are graced, accepted, and brought near because it pleased the Father to make us His own through Jesus Christ.
Ephesians 1:4 reaches even further back: “According as he hath chosen us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and without blame before him in love” (KJV).
Why are we beloved? Because He chose us. And here is the wonder: God did not send Gabriel to announce that message to us.
He sent His Son. Through the birth, death, and resurrection of Jesus, God declared that in Christ we are graced, honored, accepted, and brought near.
In the eyes of the Father, eyes that see us through His Son, we are truly accepted in the Beloved.

A Costly Yes
Still, Mary said yes. Her response remains one of the most beautiful declarations of surrender in Scripture: “I am the Lord’s servant… May your word to me be fulfilled” (Luke 1:38 NIV).

A young woman’s surrendered yes became the doorway through which the Savior entered the world.
Mary said yes to God, yes to the impossible, yes to the plan of heaven, and yes to becoming a vessel for the divine and holy. She said yes to the God who wanted to dwell within her and work through her.

Her yes meant she would become part of something larger than herself. It meant the presence of God would become a living reality in her life. It meant she would carry Jesus and, through Him, reveal the love of God to others.
Our yes to God carries that same pattern of surrender. When we yield to Him, we become carriers of His presence, witnesses of His love, and vessels through whom Christ can be seen.

What does God desire from us? The same posture He saw in Mary: a surrendered yes.

## December 11: Joseph

Joseph is one of the quietest figures in the Christmas story, yet his obedience speaks with remarkable strength. Scripture records no words from him, but his actions reveal a man who listened, trusted, protected, and obeyed.

Then God interrupted Joseph’s plan with a dream. An angel called him by his royal lineage, “Joseph son of David,” and told him not to be afraid. The child in Mary’s womb was not the result of betrayal or shame. He was conceived by the Holy Spirit.
Joseph’s obedience began in a place of surrender. He had to release the future he expected and embrace the assignment God placed before him. Loving Mary meant protecting her. Receiving Jesus meant accepting a path that would be misunderstood by others. Obedience would cost him comfort, reputation, and control.
Quiet Obedience

He led his family to Bethlehem, then later to Egypt, and then back to Nazareth. Again and again, Joseph’s life was redirected by the voice of God, and again and again, he obeyed.
Joseph reminds us that obedience is not always loud. Sometimes it looks like taking the next faithful step without applause, explanation, or certainty. Sometimes it looks like protecting what God has entrusted to you, even when others do not understand.
He was not the biological father of Jesus, yet God entrusted him with the earthly care of His Son. Joseph provided shelter, protection, leadership, and presence. He became a faithful guardian of the promise.
That is the beauty of Joseph’s place in the Christmas story. He did not need the spotlight to be significant. He did not need to be understood to be obedient. He simply listened for God’s voice and moved when God spoke.
Advent invites us to honor the Joseph places in our own lives— the quiet responsibilities, hidden sacrifices, and faithful decisions no one else may see. God sees them. God uses them. And through surrendered obedience, He makes room for Christ to be revealed.

## December 12: His Mother's Side / Father's Side

Mother’s Side / Father’s Side
Jesus took on an earthly mother so that we could be brought to our heavenly Father. In the incarnation, the eternal Son stepped fully into our humanity without ever ceasing to be fully divine.
This is the holy tension of Christmas: Jesus came through Mary’s line, yet He came from the Father’s heart. He was truly human and truly God. He entered time, yet He remained eternal. He was held in human arms, yet all creation was held together by Him.
On His mother’s side, He was a baby born in a manger; on His Father’s side, He was the Creator of the world He entered.
On His mother’s side, He was from Nazareth; on His Father’s side, He belongs to the New Jerusalem.
On His mother’s side, He lived within the limits of time; on His Father’s side, He is the Ancient of Days.
On His mother’s side, He was human; on His Father’s side, He was divine.
On His mother’s side, He took on flesh; on His Father’s side, He remained heavenly.
On His mother’s side, He rode a donkey; on His Father’s side, He rides upon the wings of the wind.
On His mother’s side, He walked dusty roads in sandals; on His Father’s side, no wall could keep Him out.
On His mother’s side, He was God incarnate; on His Father’s side, He was God Almighty.
On His mother’s side, He learned human speech and spoke with tenderness; on His Father’s side, He spoke the world into existence.
On His mother’s side, He endured pain and suffering; on His Father’s side, He triumphed over pain, suffering, sin, and death.
On His mother’s side, He was subject to His parents; on His Father’s side, He was given the name above every name.
On His mother’s side, He needed the light of a lamp; on His Father’s side, He is the light of the New Jerusalem.
On His mother’s side, He became thirsty; on His Father’s side, He offered living water that satisfies forever.
On His mother’s side, He wept at the tomb of Lazarus; on His Father’s side, He called him back to life.
On His mother’s side, He could be in one place at a time; on His Father’s side, He is present everywhere.
On His mother’s side, He was gentle as a lamb led to the slaughter; on His Father’s side, He is the Lion of the tribe of
Judah.
On His mother’s side, He was buried in a borrowed tomb; on His Father’s side, He abolished death.
On His mother’s side, His body truly died; on His Father’s side,
He rose in resurrection power.
On His mother’s side, He was born in Bethlehem; on His Father’s side, He ascended from Bethany and reigns forever.
Mary became Jesus’ earthly mother so that, through Christ, we could be brought near to our heavenly Father. The Son of Mary is also the Son of God. He came down into our story so He could lift us into the Father’s house.

### A Reflection for Today

Where do you need to remember both the nearness and the majesty of Jesus? Advent invites you to behold Him as fully human and fully divine—the One who understands your weakness and still carries all authority, power, and glory.

## December 13: The Mandate of Jesus

John 1:14 AMP: “And the Word (Christ) became flesh, and lived among us; and we [actually] saw His glory, glory as belongs to the [One and] only begotten Son of the Father, full of grace and truth.”
Jesus did not come into the world without purpose. The incarnation was not vague, sentimental, or accidental. The Word became flesh with a mandate, a divine commission, an assignment from the Father, and the authority to accomplish it.
Galatians 4:4–5 gives us the heart of that mandate: “But when the set time had fully come, God sent his Son… to redeem those under the law, that we might receive adoption to sonship” (NIV).
Why did Jesus come? To redeem us. To rescue us. To bring us back into the Father’s house.
The Mandate Was Redemption
To understand redemption, we must go back into the world of Scripture, where family, land, inheritance, and protection were deeply connected. In Hebrew, one important word for redeemer is goel. A goel was a near relative who had the responsibility to rescue, restore, repurchase, or defend a family member in need.
If someone lost land through hardship, the redeemer could buy it back. If someone was taken captive, the redeemer could bring them home. If a family member had been pushed outside the security of the household, the redeemer acted to restore what was lost.
That picture helps us understand the mission of Jesus. Humanity was not merely lost in a general sense. We were alienated from the Father’s house, separated by sin, unable to bring ourselves back. So the Father sent the Son.
This is redemption: the Redeemer comes for the one who cannot return on their own. He pays the cost, breaks the bondage, restores the inheritance, and brings the lost one back into the family.
That is why the Christmas story is not only about a baby in a manger. It is about the Son being sent on a rescue mission. Jesus came as our Redeemer, our Goel, the One who would do whatever love and justice required to bring the Father’s family home.
Psalm 103:1–4 AMP: “Bless and affectionately praise the Lord,
O my soul… Who forgives all your sins, Who heals all your diseases; Who redeems your life from the pit, Who crowns you
[lavishly] with lovingkindness and tender mercy.”
God is the Redeemer who rescues lost children and restores them to the Father’s house. Redemption is not only forgiveness from a distance; it is restoration into belonging.
Jesus’ mandate was to rescue the human family, bring us back from alienation, and restore us to the Father. He came to put us back in the family.
Jesus does more than prepare a place for us in heaven. He brings us near to the Father. The Father is not a side note in redemption;
He is the destination.
John 1:11–13 AMP: “He came to that which belonged to Him…
But to as many as did receive and welcome Him, He gave the authority, power, privilege, and right to become the children of God… They are born of God.”
The mandate of Jesus was clear: go get the Father’s family back.
That is why He came as a baby in Bethlehem. That is why He took on flesh. That is why He walked among us, suffered for us, died in our place, and rose in victory. The Redeemer came to bring us home.
Christmas is a time for family, but not only in the sentimental sense. Christmas reveals the Father’s heart to restore His family through the Son.

### A Reflection for Today

Where do you need to receive Jesus not only as Savior, but as Redeemer, the One who restores what was lost and brings you near to the Father? Advent invites you to remember that Christ did not come merely to visit humanity. He came to bring the Father’s family home.

### A Prayer for Today

(Matthew 1:21 NIV). His mission was not vague. It was salvation, rescue, restoration, and redemption.
Lord Jesus, You came as the Redeemer promised from the beginning, the Seed who would crush the serpent’s head, the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world, the One who would
“give his life as a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45 NIV). Let the power of Your redemptive purpose fill my heart with awe today.
You did not come merely to inspire; You came to deliver.
You came to break chains, restore what was lost, reclaim what was stolen, heal what was shattered, and bring humanity back to the Father. “The Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost”
(Luke 19:10 NIV). Your mandate was not simply to improve lives;
it was to redeem them.
Holy Spirit, open my eyes to see the Redeemer in every part of the Advent story. In the manger, I see the One who would carry a cross. In the swaddling clothes, I see the One who would leave an empty tomb. In the Child, I see the Savior. In His birth, I see my redemption beginning to unfold.
Father, thank You that Jesus fulfilled every part of His redemptive assignment.
He came to save.
He came to ransom.
He came to restore.
He came to redeem.
And He finished the work.
Today, let the mandate of the Redeemer awaken hope in me.
Let His victory strengthen my faith.
Let His mission shape my devotion.

## December 14: Bethlehem

Micah 5:2 KJV: “But thou, Bethlehem Ephratah, though thou be little among the thousands of Judah, yet out of thee shall he come forth unto me that is to be ruler in Israel; whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting.”
Bethlehem was a small town just south of Jerusalem. It was not known for political power, military strength, or worldly prestige.
Yet long before Mary and Joseph arrived there, God had already marked Bethlehem as the place where the Ruler of Israel would be born.
Bethlehem was also the city of David, the place connected to Israel’s shepherd-king and the royal line through which the Messiah would come. What looked small on the map was large in the purposes of God.
To this little town, Joseph and Mary came seeking shelter. The inn was full. The world had no room. Yet in that humble place,
Mary brought forth her firstborn Son, wrapped Him in swaddling clothes, and laid Him in a manger.
The House of Bread
Bethlehem is formed from two Hebrew words: Beth, meaning house, and Lehem, meaning bread. The House of Bread became the birthplace of Jesus, the Bread of Life.
Even the surrounding region, Ephrathah, carried the idea of fruitfulness. The hills around Bethlehem were known for vineyards, figs, almonds, olives, grasslands, and flocks. It was a place associated with provision, harvest, shepherding, and nourishment.
Bread was a daily necessity in the world of Scripture. It represented provision, sustenance, and life. So it is no small wonder that the One who would later say, “I am the bread of life”
(John 6:35 NIV), was born in the town whose name means House of Bread.
Even the name Bethlehem invites us to pause. The promised Bread came from the House of Bread. The One who would satisfy the hunger of the world was first laid in a feeding trough. God was preaching redemption through the place, the name, and the scene.
Bethlehem teaches us that God hides glory in humble places. He does not need the world’s approval, a palace address, or a powerful platform to fulfill His Word. He chooses the little, the overlooked, and the unexpected so that His wisdom and sovereignty shine all the brighter.
Everything about the Christmas story is rich with meaning, including the seemingly insignificant town where Jesus was born.
Bethlehem reminds us that the Bread of Life came to feed hungry souls, the Shepherd-King came from David’s city, and the eternal Ruler stepped into history through humility.

### A Reflection for Today

Where do you need to remember that God can bring eternal purpose from a small place? Advent invites you to look again at Bethlehem and trust that what seems hidden, humble, or overlooked may still be carrying the provision of God.

### A Prayer for Today

Father, thank You for Bethlehem—the small, quiet village You chose as the doorway of redemption. “But you, Bethlehem Ephrathah… out of you shall come for Me the One who will be ruler over Israel” (Micah 5:2 NKJV). You did not choose a place of power or prestige. You chose a place the world could overlook, reminding me that Your greatest works often begin in hidden places.
Lord Jesus, You entered the world in Bethlehem, the House of Bread, because You came to be the Bread of Life. You were laid in a feeding trough because You came to satisfy the hunger of every soul. Let this truth settle deeply in me today: You are the provision my heart was made for.
Father, Bethlehem was the city of David, and into that city came the Son of David—the Redeemer promised through generations.
Your sovereignty guided every step, every movement, and every detail. The census did not bring Mary and Joseph to Bethlehem by chance; Your Word brought them there by promise. Remind me that Your promises are precise, Your timing is perfect, and Your purposes are unstoppable.
Holy Spirit, help me see Bethlehem not as a sentimental scene only, but as a revelation of the Redeemer’s humility. The King of glory entered through smallness so that no one would be too low for Him to reach. Let this truth soften my heart and shape my worship. Teach me to welcome Your work even when it begins in places that feel insignificant.
Jesus, thank You for stepping into our world through Bethlehem, the humble place, the promised place, the prophetic place, and the place where redemption began to unfold in flesh and blood.
Today, let Bethlehem remind me that You come close. You come gently. You come faithfully. You come as the Bread of Life and the Redeemer who meets us where we are. Amen.

## December 15: The Star

Matthew 2:2 NKJV: “Where is He who has been born King of the Jews? For we have seen His star in the East and have come to worship Him.”
God used His own creation as a messenger. The Magi were Gentile seekers from the East, students of the sky who watched the heavens closely. In their world, unusual signs in the stars carried meaning, so God met them in a language they could recognize. They called the light “His star,” and they followed it until it led them to worship the newborn King.
In those days, the birth of a ruler was often associated with unusual heavenly phenomena. The star would have made sense to the Magi, not because the stars had power over them, but because God was using creation to point beyond itself to Christ.
If God wanted to reach these watchers of the heavens, He chose the perfect sign. He placed a message in the sky. And Scripture tells us that the sky is still preaching every night.
Psalm 19:1–3 NKJV: “The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament shows His handiwork. Day unto day utters speech, and night unto night reveals knowledge. There is no speech nor language where their voice is not heard.”
God made the stars for a purpose. They are not silent. They declare His glory, reveal His handiwork, and remind the world that creation belongs to its Creator.
During biblical times, the Hebrews used the word Mazzaroth in connection with the constellations and their seasons.
Job 38:31–32 KJV: “Canst thou bind the sweet influences of Pleiades, or loose the bands of Orion? Canst thou bring forth Mazzaroth in his season? or canst thou guide Arcturus with his sons?”
Mazzaroth is often associated with the ordered path of the constellations. The point is not that the stars rule our lives, but that God rules the stars. He set them in place, calls them by name, and uses creation to declare His glory.
Astronomy Is God-Given; Astrology Is a Corruption This distinction matters. Astronomy studies the heavens God made. Astrology turns the heavens into a source of hidden guidance, prediction, and control. Horoscopes may appear harmless, but they shift trust away from the Lord and toward created things. Scripture forbids divination, sorcery, and hidden arts because God alone is worthy of our dependence
(Deuteronomy 18:10–14).
That is why the enemy would want to twist what God created for glory into something people use for horoscopes, fear, or control.
But when creation is viewed through the truth of Scripture, the heavens do not lead us into superstition. They point us back to the Creator. They rehearse His glory and echo the story of redemption.
When these ancient constellations are viewed through a redemptive and biblical interpretation, something fascinating begins to emerge: a story that starts with a promised Seed and ends with a conquering King.
It begins with Virgo—the Virgin.
And it ends with Leo—the Lion.
In between, we see pictures that remind us of sacrifice, conflict, redemption, resurrection, living water, God's people, and ultimately the defeat of the serpent.
In other words:
The story begins with a VIRGIN bringing forth the SEED and ends with a LION crushing the SERPENT!

Virgo — The Virgin
Picture: A Virgin
Redemptive Picture: The Promised Seed Virgo means “virgin.”
And what better place to begin the redemption story than with the promise of a miraculous birth?
Isaiah prophesied:
“Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a Son...” (Isaiah 7:14).
Jesus didn't simply arrive on the scene at Bethlehem. His coming had been promised for generations.
He was conceived by the Holy Spirit, born of the Virgin Mary, and entered humanity without participating in humanity's sin.
But look at the picture associated with Virgo: The woman is traditionally pictured holding grain in one hand and a branch in the other.
One of Virgo's brightest stars is Spica, traditionally associated with an ear of grain. Immediately, our minds can go back to the first promise of redemption: THE SEED OF THE WOMAN.
Genesis 3:15 says that the Seed of the woman would ultimately crush the serpent.
Before Bethlehem...
Before Calvary...
Before the empty tomb...
God had already promised the Seed!
And then there is the Branch.
I love this because the prophets repeatedly use “Branch” as a Messianic picture.
The Branch of David (Jeremiah 23:5; 33:15). My Servant the Branch (Zechariah 3:8). The Man whose name is the Branch
(Zechariah 6:12). The Branch of the LORD (Isaiah 4:2).
So, Virgo gives us our starting point:
A Virgin. A Seed. A Branch.
And all three point our attention toward Jesus.
Libra — The Scales
Picture: Scales
Redemptive Picture: The Price Has to Be Paid After Virgo comes Libra.
Libra is pictured as a pair of scales.
Think about what scales represent: justice, equity, righteousness, something being weighed.
And here's the problem:
When humanity gets on the scales, we don't measure up.
Romans 3:23 says: “For all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God.”
Sin created a debt we couldn't pay. We couldn't balance the scales ourselves. Religion couldn't do it. Good works couldn't do it.
Trying harder couldn't do it. We needed somebody to step onto the scales for us.
And that is where the Lamb enters the story. Jesus is: “The Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world” (John 1:29).
The symbolism then points us toward the Cross. What balances the scales? THE LAMB ON THE CROSS.
We owed a debt we couldn't pay. Jesus paid a debt He didn't owe.
Justice demanded payment. Mercy provided the Lamb.
Scorpius — The Scorpion
Picture: The Scorpion
Redemptive Picture: The Conflict with the Enemy Now the story becomes a battle. Scorpius introduces the picture of the enemy. Throughout Scripture, serpents and scorpions can symbolize evil and opposition.
Jesus said:
“Behold, I give you the authority to trample on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy...” (Luke 10:19).
This part of the picture reminds us: Jesus didn't come just to be born. Jesus came to conquer.
At the Cross, it may have looked as though hell had won. Jesus was beaten. Jesus was wounded. Jesus was crucified. Jesus died.
But what looked like Satan's greatest victory became Satan's greatest defeat!
Colossians 2:15 tells us that Jesus disarmed principalities and powers and triumphed over them.
The Cross wasn't Jesus losing. The Cross was Jesus winning!
Sagittarius — The Archer
Picture: An Archer
Redemptive Picture: The Warrior Going After the
Enemy
Sagittarius is pictured as an archer with his weapon drawn.
Within this traditional interpretation, the archer is aimed toward Scorpius. The picture becomes one of conflict and victory.
There is an enemy. But there is also a Champion.
Genesis 3:15 gave us the first glimpse of this battle:
The serpent would wound His heel...but the Seed would crust the serpents head.
Don't miss the difference. The heel gets wounded. The head gets crushed. Jesus would suffer...but Satan would be defeated.
That's the Gospel!
Wounded heel. Crushed head.
Calvary wounded Jesus. But Calvary crushed the authority of the enemy.
Capricorn — The Goat
Picture: Goat/Fish
Redemptive Picture: Sacrifice and Life Capricorn is traditionally pictured as a goat with the tail of a fish.
It's a strange image until we begin thinking symbolically.
The goat immediately reminds us of sacrifice. Under the Old Covenant, goats were among the animals used in Israel's sacrificial system.
Leviticus 16 even gives us the famous picture of the scapegoat— one goat bearing away the sins of the people into the wilderness.
What a picture of Jesus!
Isaiah 53:6 says: “The LORD has laid on Him the iniquity of us all.”
He carried what belonged to us. He bore our sin. He took our judgment. And because He did...we live.
Aquarius — The Water Pourer
Picture: A Man Pouring Water
Redemptive Picture: Living Water Poured Out Now the picture changes. Aquarius shows a man pouring out a great stream of water. And throughout Scripture, water repeatedly gives us pictures of life, cleansing, baptism, and the
Holy Spirit.
Jesus stood up in John 7 and cried: “If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink.”
And then He said that out of the believer would flow rivers of living water. John tells us Jesus was speaking about the Holy
Spirit.
Think about the progression:
The Seed comes.
The price is paid.
The enemy is defeated.
And then...the Spirit is poured out!
Jesus didn't just save us FROM something. He filled us WITH
Someone.
Pisces — The Fish
Picture: Two Fish
Redemptive Picture: God's Redeemed People Pisces contains two fish connected together by a band.
Fish became one of the earliest and most recognizable symbols associated with followers of Jesus.
And Jesus told His disciples: “Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men.”
So, this picture can remind us that redemption doesn't end with us. We are caught by grace...and then sent back out to catch others!
God doesn't rescue you simply so you can sit safely in the boat.
He redeems you. He restores you. He gives you a testimony.
And then He says: “Now go tell somebody else what I did for you!”
That's evangelism.
Redeemed people become redeeming storytellers!
Aries — The Ram
Picture: A Ram
Redemptive Picture: The Sacrifice That Overcomes Aries gives us the picture of a ram. Immediately, Scripture gives us several powerful connections.
Abraham found a ram caught in the thicket that became a substitute for Isaac.
At Passover, blood marked the houses of God's people. And ultimately, all these sacrifices pointed toward Jesus.
But Revelation gives us an even greater picture. Jesus isn't merely the Lamb who was slain. He's the Lamb who was slain and is now standing!
Revelation 5 shows heaven asking:
“Who is worthy?”
And the answer is: THE LAMB! He was slain...But He didn't stay dead. The sacrifice became the Victor.
The Lamb overcame!
Taurus — The Bull
Picture: A Bull
Redemptive Picture: Strength, Service and Victory Taurus gives us the picture of tremendous strength. A bull or ox is powerful. It works. It carries. It serves. It keeps pulling.
And that gives us a beautiful picture of Jesus as the tireless
Servant.
Mark especially presents Jesus constantly moving and serving.
Jesus Himself said:
“The Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve.” (Mark
10:45).
He carried what we couldn't carry. He bore what we couldn't bear.
He finished what we couldn't finish.
And here's what I love: Jesus carries His Church. We're not carrying Him. He's carrying us!
Gemini — The Twins
Picture: Twins
Redemptive Picture: Christ's Divine and Human
Natures
Gemini is pictured as twins. This can remind us of one of the greatest mysteries of Jesus: Fully God. Fully man. Jesus wasn't
50% God and 50% man. He was truly God...and truly human.
John 1:14 says: “The Word became flesh and dwelt among us.”
God stepped into our world. He experienced hunger. Weariness.
Pain. Rejection. Tears. And ultimately death.
God didn't save humanity from a distance. He stepped into humanity to rescue us.
Gemini can also remind us of our union with Christ.
1 John 3:2 says: “When He is revealed, we shall be like Him, for we shall see Him as He is.”
He's not only bringing us OUT of something. He's transforming us INTO something.
Cancer — The Gathering
Picture: Traditionally a Crab
Redemptive Picture: The Gathering of the Redeemed Cancer can be understood in this redemptive interpretation as a picture of gathering and holding securely.
And that makes me think about Jesus'words:
“I will build My church.” — Matthew 16:18 Jesus didn't just come for isolated individuals. He came to gather a people.
A family.
A body.
A Church.
People from every tribe...
Every tongue...
Every nation...
Every background...
All gathered around one throne, worshiping one King.
The Gospel doesn't just reconcile us to God. It creates a FAMILY.
Leo — The Lion
Picture: The Lion
Redemptive Picture: THE KING
And now we reach the end.
Leo. THE LION.
The story began with Virgo—the Virgin.
Now it ends with Leo—the Lion.
Jesus is called: “The Lion of the tribe of Judah.” — Revelation 5:5 One of the brightest stars in Leo is Regulus, a name historically associated with royalty or kingship.
And beneath the Lion stretches Hydra, the serpent.
What a final picture. Genesis began with a serpent...Revelation ends with the serpent defeated.
Genesis 3:15 promised that the Seed of the woman would crush the serpent's head.
And now the whole story has come full circle.
The Virgin brought forth the Seed.
The Seed became the Lamb.
The Lamb went to the Cross.
The Cross defeated the enemy.
The Lamb rose again.
The Spirit was poured out.
The redeemed were gathered.
And now...
THE LION REIGNS!
The enemy doesn't get the final word. Sin doesn't get the final word. Death doesn't get the final word. Hell doesn't get the final word.
JESUS DOES!

We don't worship the stars. We worship the ONE WHO MADE

That's the message.

And creation keeps declaring: THERE IS A CREATOR.
Before the written Word, there was the spoken sky. And when we view these ancient pictures through the lens of redemption, they give us a beautiful illustration of the Gospel:
A Virgin.

A Lamb sacrificed.
An enemy defeated.
Living water poured out.

The point is this: the heavens were created to declare the glory of God, not to become horoscopes for predicting our future. ### A Reflection for Today

Where are you tempted to look for guidance apart from God?
Advent invites you to follow the light He gives all the way to Jesus.
The stars may declare His glory, but only Christ can save, guide, and satisfy the soul.

## December 16: The Manger

Long before Mary and Joseph arrived in Bethlehem, God had already spoken through the prophet Micah. Seven hundred years before the birth of Jesus, Micah named the place where the Messiah would enter the world.
Micah 5:2 ESV: “But you, O Bethlehem Ephrathah, who are too little to be among the clans of Judah, from you shall come forth for me one who is to be ruler in Israel, whose coming forth is from of old, from ancient days.”
This means the Father had not only chosen the city, but had woven every detail of Christ’s coming into His eternal plan. The incarnation unfolded at the right time, in the right place, and in the right way.
Then Luke shows us the surprising sign God placed at the center of that holy night:
Luke 2:7 ESV: “And she gave birth to her firstborn son and wrapped him in swaddling cloths and laid him in a MANGER, because there was no place for them in the inn.”
Luke 2:12 ESV: “And this will be a sign for you: you will find a baby wrapped in swaddling cloths and lying in a MANGER.”
Luke 2:16 ESV: “And they went with haste and found Mary and Joseph, and the baby lying in a MANGER.”
Luke is the only Gospel writer who mentions the manger, and he mentions it three times in just a few verses. That repetition invites us to slow down and pay attention.
Why would Luke make sure we knew about Jesus’ first bed?
The Sign of the Manger
The Greek word translated manger is phatnē, a feeding place for animals. It is connected to the idea of eating. The first bed of Jesus was not a cradle of comfort or a royal bassinet. It was a feeding trough.
That detail carries deep prophetic beauty. The Child born in Bethlehem, the House of Bread, was laid in a place where creatures came to be fed.
Jesus was born in Bethlehem, a town whose name means “House of Bread.”
Later, after multiplying loaves of bread, Jesus would say, “I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will never go hungry”
(John 6:35 NIV).
He was not speaking merely of physical bread. He was revealing Himself as the One who satisfies the deepest hunger of the human soul.
At the Last Supper, Jesus took bread and said, “This is my body given for you” (Luke 22:19 NIV). The Bread of Life would give Himself for the life of the world.
The manger, the feeding trough, was a sign of what Jesus came to do. He came to offer Himself as bread for our souls. He came to satisfy a hunger that could not be satisfied any other way.
We often picture the manger as wooden, but in the ancient world it may also have been stone, carved to hold feed for animals.
Either way, the meaning remains stunning: the King of glory was laid in a humble feeding place.
Every part of the Christmas story carries meaning. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is random. The manger shows us that Jesus came low enough for all to come near and generous enough to give Himself as the food our souls desperately need.

Advent invites you to come to the manger and behold the Bread of Life, the One who meets us in humility, feeds us with grace, and gives Himself fully for our redemption.

“Though the Lord is exalted, he looks kindly on the lowly” (Psalm
138:6 NIV).

## December 17: Swaddling Clothes

Luke 2:7 KJV: “And she brought forth her firstborn son, and wrapped him in swaddling clothes, and laid him in a manger;
because there was no room for them in the inn.”
Luke is the only Gospel writer who tells us that Jesus was wrapped in swaddling clothes. He mentions the manger three times, but he also makes sure we see the cloths wrapped around the newborn Savior.
That detail may seem small, but Scripture often hides deep meaning in small details. Swaddling clothes speak of tenderness, care, humility, and humanity. The eternal Son entered the world dependent enough to be wrapped by human hands.
There is another striking reference to swaddling in Ezekiel 16:3–
4. There, the prophet compares Israel to an abandoned infant who had not been washed, cared for, or wrapped. The image is heartbreaking: a child left exposed, uncared for, and vulnerable.
Against that background, Luke’s description becomes even more beautiful. Jesus was not abandoned. He was received. He was wrapped. He was held. Mary’s hands covered the One who had come to cover the world with salvation.
The words connected to swaddling carry the idea of wrapping or binding with strips of cloth. In practical terms, swaddling helped protect and steady a newborn child. But in the Christmas story, the image reaches beyond practicality. It shows us the humility of Christ’s coming.
Wrapped in Humility
Some traditions connect the swaddling cloths with strips of priestly garments or cloths used by shepherds to protect sacrificial lambs. While Scripture does not give every historical detail, the imagery is powerful and worth pondering carefully: the Lamb of God entered the world wrapped in humility, tenderness, and purpose.
The shepherds would have understood the language of lambs, sacrifice, and inspection. They knew what it meant for a lamb to be protected, watched, and set apart. So when the angel gave them the sign—a baby wrapped in swaddling clothes and lying in a manger—it was not random. Heaven had chosen a sign they could recognize.
Can you imagine the moment they found Him? A baby in Bethlehem. A manger. Swaddling clothes. Shepherds who cared for lambs now stood before the Lamb of God. The Great Shepherd had come among the shepherds. God was connecting every detail with astonishing precision.
There is another beautiful possibility to consider. If Jesus was wrapped in cloths connected to priestly service, then the image becomes even richer: the Great High Priest began His earthly life wrapped in humble cloths, already pointing toward the sacrifice He would one day offer.
Our Great High Priest, Wrapped in Humility Hebrews 4:14–16 NLT: “So then, since we have a great High Priest who has entered heaven, Jesus the Son of God, let us hold firmly to what we believe. This High Priest of ours understands our weaknesses, for he faced all of the same testings we do, yet he did not sin. So let us come boldly to the throne of our gracious God. There we will receive his mercy, and we will find grace to help us when we need it most.”
The swaddling clothes remind us that God is in the details. The Savior did not arrive in random symbols. He came wrapped in signs of humility, sacrifice, priesthood, tenderness, and nearness.
The One who would one day be wrapped in burial cloths began His life wrapped in the cloths of infancy.

## December 18: Shepherds

Luke 2:8–12 NIV: “And there were shepherds living out in the fields nearby, keeping watch over their flocks at night. An angel of the Lord appeared to them, and the glory of the Lord shone around them… But the angel said to them, ‘Do not be afraid. I bring you good news that will cause great joy for all the people…
This will be a sign to you: You will find a baby wrapped in cloths and lying in a manger.’”
Why were shepherds the first to hear the announcement of Jesus’ birth?
Luke tells us they were living out in the fields near Bethlehem, keeping watch over their flocks at night. They were ordinary men doing ordinary work when heaven broke open above them. Yet in the wisdom of God, these shepherds were not incidental to the story.
The First Witnesses
Bethlehem was closely connected to shepherding and sacrifice.
Some traditions associate the fields near Bethlehem with Migdal Eder, the “Tower of the Flock,” mentioned in Micah 4:8. Whether or not every historical detail can be proven with certainty, the symbolism is deeply meaningful: shepherds near Bethlehem were the first to hear that the Lamb of God had come.
Micah 4:8 KJV: “And thou, O tower of the flock, the strong hold of the daughter of Zion, unto thee shall it come, even the first dominion; the kingdom shall come to the daughter of
Jerusalem.”
The phrase Tower of the Flock gives us a beautiful picture. A tower was a place of watchfulness. A flock was a place of care.
Shepherds stood between danger and the lambs entrusted to them. They watched through the night, protected what was vulnerable, and remained attentive while others slept.
If these shepherds were connected in any way to the care of lambs used for temple sacrifice, the sign given by the angel becomes even more striking. They knew lambs. They knew watching, guarding, inspecting, and protecting. So heaven sent them to find a baby wrapped in cloths and lying in a manger.
The angel could have appeared to rulers, scholars, or priests in Jerusalem. Instead, the glory of the Lord shone around shepherds in a field. The first announcement of the Savior’s birth came to those who understood the language of lambs.
That matters because Jesus would not only be called a shepherd;
He would also be revealed as the Lamb. John the Baptist would one day point to Him and say, “Look, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!” (John 1:29 NIV).
The shepherds were invited to see what generations had longed to see. The Lamb had come. The Shepherd had come. The One who would lay down His life for the sheep was lying in a manger, wrapped in humility.
Scripture returns to this image again and again. Isaiah saw the Suffering Servant as a lamb led to the slaughter. John identified Jesus as the Lamb of God. Peter called Him a lamb without blemish or defect. Revelation worships Him as the Lamb who was slain and now reigns forever.
The shepherds’ visit was more than a tender detail in the Christmas story. It was a living sign. Those who watched over lambs were sent to behold the Lamb. Those who guarded vulnerable creatures were invited to worship the Savior who had made Himself vulnerable for us.
The Lamb of God had come to take away the sin of the world, and His first earthly visitors were shepherds.
God does not waste details. He writes redemption with precision, tenderness, and love. The fields, the flock, the night watch, the angel, the sign, and the manger all point us back to Jesus.

## December 19: Heavenly Hosts

Luke 2:13–14 NASB: “And suddenly there appeared with the angel a multitude of the heavenly host praising God and saying,

Can you imagine the night sky suddenly filled with angels?
One angel had announced the birth of the Savior to the shepherds. Then, suddenly, the heavens opened wider. A multitude of the heavenly host appeared, praising God and filling the night with worship.
The word host carries the idea of a multitude, a great company, or an army. When Scripture speaks of the heavenly host, it often points to the angelic armies of God—holy servants who carry out His commands, worship before His throne, and move at His direction.
The Bible sometimes describes angels with military language:
they encamp, obey commands, stand in ranks, and engage in spiritual conflict. Heaven is not disorganized. The hosts of heaven serve the Lord with order, strength, and worship.
So, when the heavenly host appeared over Bethlehem, it was not a small choir drifting quietly through the sky. It was the army of heaven erupting in praise because the King had come.
The Army of Heaven
And if there is an army, there is a Commander.
One of the names of God is Lord of Hosts, or Yahweh Sabaoth, the Lord of heaven’s armies. This name appears throughout the Old Testament and reveals God as the sovereign Commander over every spiritual power, every angelic host, and every battle that belongs to Him.
That makes Bethlehem even more astonishing. The Lord of Hosts did not arrive with earthly armies, weapons, or a palace guard. He came as a baby in the House of Bread, laid in a manger, wrapped in swaddling clothes, and announced by heaven’s armies.
The people of Jesus’ day expected a Messiah who would overthrow Rome by force. But Jesus came to fight a deeper battle.
He came to defeat sin, death, hell, and the powers of darkness.
His battlefield would lead through Gethsemane, the cross, and the grave.
In Bethlehem, the Bread of Life entered the world. At the cross, the Warrior-King fought the battle our souls could never win. He conquered by surrender, triumphed through sacrifice, and brought peace through His blood.
Why did the heavenly hosts show up? Because the Commander of heaven’s armies had stepped into time. The King had come, and heaven knew what earth was only beginning to understand.
The hosts of heaven appeared to accompany the announcement of their King. The Lord of Hosts, Jehovah Sabaoth, could be found not in a fortress, but in a stable; not on a battlefield, but in a manger.
The Lord of Hosts, the King of Glory, had arrived.
Psalm 24:7–10 NIV: “Lift up your heads, you gates… that the King of glory may come in. Who is this King of glory? The Lord strong and mighty, the Lord mighty in battle… The Lord Almighty—he is the King of glory.”
The heavenly hosts were not merely filling the sky with sound.
They were announcing that the King of Glory had come near. The armies of heaven praised the Prince of Peace. The Commander of angel armies came as a Child to win the war that only love could win.

## December 20: The Wise Men

The wise men came from the East because they had seen a star, but they were looking for more than a sign in the sky. They were searching for a King.
Matthew calls them magi, wise men from the East who came to Jerusalem asking, “Where is the one who has been born king of the Jews?” They had seen His star and had come for one purpose:
to worship Him.
Over time, tradition has often pictured three kings at the manger.
Scripture is more restrained. Matthew does not tell us their names, their exact number, or that they were kings. What he does tell us is enough: these Gentile seekers recognized that heaven was pointing to a Jewish King, and they were willing to travel until they found Him.
Their journey brought them first to Jerusalem, where their question troubled Herod and stirred the city. The arrival of these foreign worshipers exposed a holy contrast: those who seemed far away were seeking Jesus, while those geographically near had to be told where the Messiah would be born.
Matthew 2:1–3 NIV: “After Jesus was born in Bethlehem in Judea, during the time of King Herod, Magi from the east came to Jerusalem and asked, ‘Where is the one who has been born king of the Jews? We saw his star when it rose and have come to worship him.’ When King Herod heard this he was disturbed, and all Jerusalem with him.”
Seekers from the Nations
Their arrival was a sign that the hope of Israel was never meant for Israel alone. The nations were being drawn to the light of Christ. Long before the Magi arrived, Scripture had pictured Gentiles bringing gifts and nations coming to the brightness of God’s rising glory.
Isaiah 60:3, 6 NIV: “Nations will come to your light, and kings to the brightness of your dawn… And all from Sheba will come, bearing gold and incense and proclaiming the praise of the
Lord.”
When the Magi finally found the Child, they opened their treasures and presented three gifts rich with meaning:
• Gold honored Jesus as King.
• Frankincense pointed to worship, priesthood, and the fragrance of offering.
• Myrrh foreshadowed suffering, burial, and the sacrificial death Jesus would one day die.
Their gifts were not random. They became a prophetic testimony in treasure form. Before Jesus preached a sermon, healed a body, or raised the dead, the Magi’s gifts were already declaring who He was: King, Priest, and Sacrifice.
A Long Obedience of Seeking
The identity of the Magi has been discussed for centuries. They may have been connected to the learned classes of the East—men trained in wisdom, astronomy, interpretation, and royal counsel.
Because Daniel served in Babylon and was placed over wise men in that realm, many believers have wondered whether his prophetic influence helped preserve expectation for the coming Messiah among eastern seekers.
Daniel 2:48 says that after God gave Daniel the interpretation of Nebuchadnezzar’s dream, the king “made him ruler over the entire province of Babylon and placed him in charge of all its wise men” (NIV). Daniel’s faithful witness stood in the very world from which later eastern wise men may have emerged.
Scripture does not tell us exactly how much the Magi knew or how their knowledge was preserved. But it does show us this: God is able to plant truth in one generation and bring fruit from it centuries later.
Daniel also received a prophecy about the timing of Messiah’s coming (Daniel 9:24–26). We cannot say with certainty that the Magi were reading Daniel’s writings, but we can say that God’s Word outlives empires, survives exile, and continues working long after the first hearers are gone.
The Magi remind us that God can guide seekers across generations, nations, and distances. Their story is not only about a star in the sky. It is about a God who knows how to lead hungry hearts all the way to Jesus.
When the fullness of time came, they packed treasures, followed the light they had been given, entered the house, saw the Child with Mary His mother, bowed down, and worshiped Him.
Wisdom became worship. Searching became surrender. Distance became devotion.
History is truly His story. God was weaving promise, prophecy, empire, exile, stars, Scripture, and seekers into one breathtaking moment: Gentiles from the East bowing before Israel’s Messiah.

## December 21: The Good News

Luke 2:10–11 ESV: “And the angel said to them, ‘Fear not, for behold, I bring you GOOD NEWS of great joy that will be for all the people. For unto you is born this day in the city of David a Savior, who is Christ the Lord.’”
The angel’s announcement was more than comforting words in a dark field. It was royal news. Heaven was declaring that a Savior had been born, the true King had come, and joy was now available for all people.
The word translated “good news” is closely connected to the word gospel. In Greek, euangelion means good news, glad tidings, or a joyful announcement. By the time Jesus was born, it was a word people in the Roman world would have recognized.
In the Greco-Roman world, euangelion could be used for major public announcements—especially news connected to rulers, victories, imperial power, or events believed to change the course of society.
That makes Luke’s wording remarkable. The angel was not announcing the good news of Rome. He was announcing the good news of God. Not the birth of Caesar. Not the triumph of an empire. Not peace enforced by military power. The angel proclaimed the birth of Jesus, the Savior, Christ the Lord.
Caesar Augustus ruled the Roman Empire when Jesus was born.
Rome claimed to bring peace, order, and salvation to the world.
Imperial inscriptions even used language of “good news” in connection with Augustus and his reign.
One famous inscription from Priene, dated around 9 B.C., referred to the birthday of Augustus as the beginning of good news for the world. The language was bold: Rome wanted people to see Caesar as a savior, bringer of peace, and center of history.
But Luke places another announcement beside Rome’s announcement. While Caesar’s empire claimed power, heaven announced a Child. While Rome celebrated the emperor’s birthday, angels celebrated the birth of the true Savior. While Caesar’s peace depended on force, Christ came to bring peace through redemption.
To call Jesus’ birth good news was therefore a direct and holy contrast. The gospel of Rome said, Caesar is lord. The gospel of heaven said, Jesus is Lord. Rome promised peace through domination. Jesus brought peace through humility, sacrifice, and resurrection.
That contrast is astonishing. In one corner of the empire, Caesar issued a decree that moved people across the Roman world. In another corner, a young couple traveled to Bethlehem, and the true King entered history unnoticed by the powerful.
Caesar thought he was counting subjects. God was fulfilling prophecy.
Luke 2:1 NIV: “In those days Caesar Augustus issued a decree that a census should be taken of the entire Roman world.”
That decree sent Joseph and Mary to Bethlehem at the very time Mary was ready to give birth. Caesar never intended to fulfill Micah’s prophecy. He never knew Mary’s name. He never understood that his census would serve the purposes of the God who rules over kings.
On one silent, holy night, unnoticed in a corner of Caesar’s kingdom, the King of kings was born. Not in Rome. Not in a palace. Not beneath banners of empire. In Bethlehem. In humility. In a manger.
The Prince of Peace had come to establish a kingdom Rome could not build and death could not destroy.
Caesar Augustus is long gone, but the kingdom announced in Bethlehem has no end. Rome’s glory faded. Christ’s reign continues. The angel’s good news still outlasts every empire.
Mark 1:1 NIV: “The beginning of the good news about Jesus the Messiah, the Son of God.”
Mark 1:14–15 NIV: “Jesus went into Galilee, proclaiming the good news of God. ‘The time has come,’ he said. ‘The kingdom of God has come near. Repent and believe the good news!’”
Matthew 24:14 NIV: “And this gospel of the kingdom will be preached in the whole world as a testimony to all nations, and then the end will come.”
Romans 1:16 NIV: “For I am not ashamed of the gospel, because it is the power of God that brings salvation to everyone who believes.”
Isaiah had already described this kind of announcement centuries before Jesus came:
Isaiah 52:7 NIV: “How beautiful on the mountains are the feet of those who bring good news, who proclaim peace, who bring good tidings, who proclaim salvation, who say to Zion, ‘Your God reigns!’”
This is the good news of Christmas: God reigns, the Savior has come, and His kingdom is not built on force, fear, or earthly power. It is built on grace, truth, redemption, and peace. The angel’s announcement in Bethlehem still confronts every false gospel with the glory of the true one: Jesus Christ is Lord.

Advent invites you to receive again the announcement heaven gave the shepherds: a Savior has been born, joy is available for all people, and the true King has come near.

Lord Jesus, You are the good news. Your birth is good news. Your life is good news. Your cross is good news. Your resurrection is good news. Everything about You carries hope, healing, and redemption. The angels did not proclaim a concept; they proclaimed a Person. Let my heart receive You with the same wonder the shepherds felt when heaven broke into their night.
Holy Spirit, let the good news become more than familiar words to me. Let it become living truth. Let it be power, light, and hope in every dark place of my heart. The gospel is not advice; it is announcement. It is not human effort; it is divine rescue. Open my eyes to the beauty of the message that changed the world.
Father, the good news is for all people—for the broken, the weary, the wandering, the fearful, and the forgotten. It is for those who feel unworthy and those who feel unseen. It reaches every place the human heart can go. Let this widen my compassion and deepen my gratitude.
Jesus, thank You that the good news is not that we found our way to You, but that You came to us. Not that we earned salvation, but that You gave it. Not that we climbed to heaven, but that heaven came down.

## December 22: Wonderful Counselor

Jesus is _____. How would you fill in that blank?
• Teacher
• Best Friend
• Everything
• Provider
• Father
• Holy One
• Redeemer
All of these are beautiful answers. Each one reveals something true about who He is and how He meets us.
But Isaiah gives us four names that open the identity of the Messiah with remarkable depth: Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, and Prince of Peace.
Isaiah 9:6–7 NKJV: “For unto us a Child is born, unto us a Son is given; and the government will be upon His shoulder. And His name will be called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God,
Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace. Of the increase of His government and peace there will be no end… The zeal of the Lord of hosts will perform this.”
Isaiah wrote these words centuries before Christ was born, yet the names he recorded still speak with living power. They reveal the character of the Child who would come and the help He still brings to us today.
These four names speak to our deepest needs: wisdom, power, belonging, and peace.
Jesus Is Wonderful Counselor
The title Wonderful Counselor carries the idea of a wonder of a counselor—One whose wisdom is astonishing, extraordinary, and beyond human limitation. He does not merely offer good advice.
He reveals the heart, mind, and will of God.
Jeremiah 29:11 NIV: “For I know the plans I have for you,”
declares the Lord, “plans to prosper you and not to harm you, plans to give you hope and a future.”
The word wonderful points to what is miraculous, astonishing, and too great to be reduced to human understanding. The word counselor points to one who gives wisdom, direction, strategy, and guidance. Together, the title tells us that Jesus is the divine Counselor whose wisdom never fails and whose guidance never leads us astray.
Human counselors can offer insight, encouragement, and help, but even the wisest human voice is limited. Jesus is different. He does not need counsel from anyone. He knows the end from the beginning, the condition of every heart, and the path that leads to life.
When we come to Him, we do not come to a distant advisor or an uncertain guide. We come to the One who is wisdom Himself. He knows what we need before we ask, and He is faithful to lead us in truth.
Psalm 32:8 NIV: “I will instruct you and teach you in the way you should go; I will counsel you with my loving eye on you.”
Psalm 33:11 NIV: “But the plans of the Lord stand firm forever, the purposes of his heart through all generations.”
This gives us confidence in the way He works. His plans are not shallow. His counsel is not reactive. His wisdom is not shaped by panic, pressure, or limited perspective. He sees what we cannot see, knows what we cannot know, and accomplishes what we could never bring to pass on our own.
As Wonderful Counselor, Jesus gives wholesome direction to His people. Those who follow Him do not have to walk in darkness.
He leads with wisdom, watches with love, corrects with mercy, and guides with perfect faithfulness.
So how do we fill in the blank? Jesus is Wonderful Counselor.
• He has never seen a problem He could not solve.
• He has never seen a hurt He could not heal.
• He has never seen a battle He could not win.
• He has never seen a question He could not answer.
• He has never seen an obligation He could not meet.
• He has never seen a failure He could not redeem.
• He has never seen a loss He could not restore.
Advent reminds us that the Child in the manger is not only Savior and King; He is the Wonderful Counselor who still speaks into confusion, fear, grief, decisions, relationships, and every hidden place where we need divine wisdom.

He is present, wise, and faithful to guide you with love.

## December 23: Mighty God

Jesus is _____. How would you fill in that blank?
Isaiah 9:6–7 NKJV: “For unto us a Child is born, unto us a Son is given; and the government will be upon His shoulder. And His name will be called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God,
Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace. Of the increase of His government and peace there will be no end… The zeal of the Lord of hosts will perform this.”
The second name Isaiah gives the Messiah is Mighty God. This title reveals more than strength in a general sense. It declares that the Child born to us is fully divine, fully powerful, and fully able to accomplish every purpose of God.
This is no ordinary child. As the ancient creeds confess, Jesus is truly God. The fullness of deity dwells in Him, and the power of heaven rests upon Him without limit.
The word translated mighty carries the idea of strength, valor, and victorious power. It can describe a warrior, champion, or strong one. Isaiah is not presenting a fragile Savior who merely sympathizes from a distance. He is revealing the God who has power to act, power to save, and power to overcome.
Jesus is the Mighty God—the divine Champion who fights for His people. He does not only understand our battles; He enters them with authority. He confronts sin, defeats darkness, breaks bondage, and makes a way where no human strength could ever prevail.
Psalm 45:3 NLT: “Put on your sword, O mighty warrior! You are so glorious, so majestic!”
He is the God who fights for His people.
The Power to Accomplish the Plan
When we hold Isaiah’s first two titles together, we see a beautiful progression. As Wonderful Counselor, Jesus knows the plan. As Mighty God, He has the power to accomplish it.
The One who gives perfect counsel also carries perfect power. His wisdom is not empty theory, and His plans are not wishful thinking. What He purposes, He is able to perform.
All of His wonderful plans are carried out with divine might. The omnipotence of God is not outside His reach; it belongs to Him.
Whatever He desires, He is able to achieve. Whatever He promises, He is able to fulfill. Whatever He redeems, He is able to restore.
This matters for Advent because the Child in the manger is also the Mighty God. The One wrapped in swaddling clothes holds all power. The One laid in a manger upholds the universe. The One who came in humility reigns with authority.
So how do we fill in the blank? Jesus is Mighty God.

### A Reflection for Today

Where do you need to remember that Jesus is not only wise, but mighty? Advent invites you to bring Him the battles you cannot win, the burdens you cannot carry, and the impossibilities you cannot solve. The Child born to us is Mighty God, and His strength is never exhausted.

## December 24: Everlasting Father

Isaiah 9:6–7 NKJV: “For unto us a Child is born, unto us a Son is given; and the government will be upon His shoulder. And His name will be called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God,
Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace. Of the increase of His government and peace there will be no end… The zeal of the Lord of hosts will perform this.”
Jesus is _____. How would you fill in that blank?
Jesus Is Everlasting Father
The third name Isaiah gives the Messiah is Everlasting Father.
In Hebrew, the phrase can be understood as Father of Eternity.
It does not mean the Son is the same Person as God the Father;
rather, it reveals the Messiah’s eternal nature, fatherly care, and life-giving authority toward His people.
In Scripture, fatherhood often carries the ideas of source, protection, provision, leadership, and compassionate care. A good father guards what has been entrusted to him. He provides, guides, corrects, covers, and loves with steadfast commitment.
The title also carries royal meaning. In the ancient world, a righteous king was often expected to care for his people with fatherlike protection. Isaiah is showing us a King whose care is not temporary, limited, or fragile. His reign is everlasting, and His compassion does not expire.
Jesus reveals the Father perfectly. Paul says He is “the image of the invisible God” (Colossians 1:15 NIV). He is not merely a reflection from a distance; He is God made visible. In Him, the heart, holiness, mercy, and glory of God are revealed in human flesh.
That means when we look at Jesus, we see what the Father is like.
We see compassion touching lepers, mercy restoring sinners, truth confronting darkness, and love moving toward the broken.
Jesus shows us that the eternal God is not cold, distant, or indifferent. He is near, tender, holy, and full of steadfast love.
Because He is the Father of Eternity, He does not merely comfort us for a moment; He gives eternal life. He does not merely visit our need; He brings us into everlasting belonging. His care reaches backward before the foundation of the world and forward into the ages to come.
Jeremiah 31:3 NIV: “I have loved you with an everlasting love; I have drawn you with unfailing kindness.”
Psalm 103:13–14 NIV: “As a father has compassion on his children, so the Lord has compassion on those who fear him; for he knows how we are formed, he remembers that we are dust.”
This is the tenderness Isaiah’s title helps us behold. The Child born to us is not only wise and mighty; He is eternally compassionate toward His people. He remembers our frame. He knows our weakness. He draws near with a love that does not fade.
There is nothing temporary about His care. Earthly love can falter.
Human protection can fail.
Time can change circumstances, relationships, strength, and seasons. But Jesus remains the same yesterday, today, and forever.
So how do we fill in the blank? Jesus is Everlasting Father.
Advent reminds us that the Child in the manger carries eternal fatherly care. He is the One who gathers, protects, provides, restores, and holds His people with everlasting love.

### A Reflection for Today

Advent invites you to rest in the One whose love does not age, whose compassion does not run out, and whose fatherly care holds you securely through every season.

## December 25: Prince of Peace

Isaiah 9:6–7 NKJV: “For unto us a Child is born, unto us a Son is given; and the government will be upon His shoulder. And His name will be called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God,
Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace. Of the increase of His government and peace there will be no end… The zeal of the Lord of hosts will perform this.”
The fourth name Isaiah gives the Messiah is Prince of Peace. This final title gathers the beauty of all the others and brings them to rest. The Wonderful Counselor brings wisdom. The Mighty God brings power. The Everlasting Father brings eternal care. And the Prince of Peace brings wholeness, reconciliation, and rest.
Peace is not merely the absence of conflict. In Scripture, peace carries the idea of wholeness, completeness, well-being, harmony with God, and restoration of what has been broken. The peace Jesus brings reaches deeper than circumstances and lasts longer than any earthly calm.
The Hebrew word translated prince is often connected with rule, leadership, and authority. This means Jesus does not merely offer peace as a feeling; He reigns as the Prince whose authority establishes peace. His peace is not fragile, sentimental, or temporary. It is the peace of a King whose kingdom cannot be shaken.
We live in a world that longs for peace but cannot manufacture it.
Nations pursue treaties. Families try to repair wounds. Hearts search for rest. Yet human peace often depends on favorable conditions, controlled outcomes, or temporary relief.
Isaiah shows us that God’s plan for peace is not centered in a policy, a system, or an empire. God’s plan for peace is a Person.
His name is Jesus.
• In the past, His coming made peace with God.
• In the present, those who come to Him receive peace in their hearts.
• In the future, His return will usher in a kingdom of peace without end.
This is why Christmas speaks so powerfully to anxious hearts. The Child in the manger came to reconcile us to God. The One called Prince of Peace would go to the cross, absorb our sin, break down the wall of separation, and make peace through His blood.
Jesus does not simply calm storms around us; He speaks peace within us. He gives a peace the world cannot give and cannot take away.
John 14:27 NIV: “Peace I leave with you; my peace I give you. I do not give to you as the world gives. Do not let your hearts be troubled and do not be afraid.”
He came to bring peace. He did. He does. He will.
So how do we fill in the blank? Jesus is Prince of Peace.
Think of Isaiah’s names this way:
The Wonderful Counselor knows the way.
The Mighty God has power to make the way.
The Everlasting Father holds us along the way.
The Prince of Peace brings us home in wholeness and rest.

### A Reflection for Today

Where do you need the peace of Christ to rule today? Christmas invites you to receive the Prince of Peace not only as the Child born in Bethlehem, but as the King who reconciles you to God, guards your heart, and brings wholeness where life has been fractured.
